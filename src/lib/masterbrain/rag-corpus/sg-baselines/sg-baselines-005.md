**2.2.1 SG:Off-the-Tee** SG:Off-the-Tee encompasses all tee shots on par 4s and par 5s, evaluating both distance and accuracy through their joint effect on the resulting position’s expected strokes. This com- ponent captures what traditional driving statistics cannot: the trade-off between distance and accuracy under specific course conditions. A 280-yard drive in the fairway gains substantially more than a 250-yard drive in the fairway because it leaves a shorter approach with lower expected strokes; a 300-yard drive in light rough may gain or lose strokes relative to a shorter fairway drive depending on rough severity and remaining distance. The computational implementation requires mapping each drive’s outcome—distance and lie—to the appropriate baseline. On a 460-yard par 4, a 280-yard fairway drive (leaving 180 yards, expected strokes
**3.08) yields SG = 4.17 − 3.08 − 1 = +0.09. The same distance in rough (expected strokes 3.31) yields** SG = 4.17 − 3.31 − 1 = −0.14—a 0.23-stroke differential from fairway versus rough that illustrates how SG automatically integrates both distance and accuracy effects.
**2.2.2 SG:Approach-the-Green** SG:Approach-the-Green includes all shots aimed at the green from the fairway, rough, sand, or tee (on par 3s), encompassing the full range of iron and hybrid play. This component emerges as the single largest source of scoring separation between players at all skill levels—a finding that challenges traditional coaching emphases on short game and putting. The dominance of approach play reflects the compounding of frequency and impact. Elite players face **14–18 approach opportunities per round (including par 3s), each with substantial SG variance. A** tour professional gaining 0.1 strokes per approach accumulates 1.4–1.8 strokes per round—often the margin between winning and missing cuts. For amateurs, the differential is magnified: high-handicap players not only miss greens more frequently but miss them by larger margins when they do hit, creating cascading score inflation through recovery shots and long putts. Tiger Woods’s career dominance derived primarily from approach play excellence—hitting 75% of greens **from 150–200 yards versus tour average of ~67%, generating approximately 1.28 strokes per round** of total advantage. This pattern persists across eras: the top-10 players in total SG from 2004–2012 derived two-thirds **of their scoring advantage from shots outside 100 yards, with approach** shots contributing the largest share.
**2.2.3 SG:Around-the-Green** SG:Around-the-Green captures shots within approximately 30 yards of the green edge that are not on the putting surface—chips, pitches, bunker shots, and flop shots. While traditionally emphasized in golf instruction (“short game is where scores are made”), Broadie’s data reveals this component as secondary to long-game proficiency for most players. The category’s smaller impact on scoring separation reflects diminished opportunity: even elite play- ers face only 5–10 such shots per round, limiting total SG variance. However, for players with specific short-game weaknesses, this component can represent the largest single improvement opportunity. At amateur levels, where green misses are more frequent, Around-the-Green assumes greater relative impor- tance—though still typically trailing Approach and Off-Tee in absolute stroke impact.
