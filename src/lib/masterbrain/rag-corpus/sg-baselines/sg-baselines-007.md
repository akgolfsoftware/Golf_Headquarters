2.4.3 Putting Research: “Putt for Dough” Debunked Broadie’s analysis reveals putting as the
least important of the four SG components for separating elite players, contradicting decades of golf
conventional wisdom. The evidence from correlation analysis:

| SG Component | Correlation with Scoring Average (Historical) |
| --- | --- |
| SG:Approach | ~0.70 |
| SG:Off-the-Tee | ~0.50 |
| SG:Around-the-Green | ~0.40 |
| SG:Putting | ~0.35 |

Putting shows the weakest relationship to overall scoring, with the smallest standard deviation between
elite and average performers. The explanation: putting outcomes incorporate substantial randomness
(lip-outs, green imperfections, wind gusts), requiring large samples to distinguish skill from luck. Even
elite putters face limited high-leverage opportunities (few 15–25 footers per round), and putting performance shows stronger regression to mean than other components, suggesting larger luck component.

For amateur golfers, the putting differential remains relatively small compared to approach and driving. A
20-handicap player loses approximately 2–4 strokes per round to the PGA Tour in putting, compared
to 7–11 in approach. The coaching priority is therefore clear: putting practice should occupy
proportionally less training time than its psychological salience would suggest, except for
players with specific technical deficiencies (e.g., yips, poor speed control on lag putts).

2.4.4 Amateur Data: Where Amateurs Lose Strokes vs. Professionals Broadie’s amateur
database, collected through Golf Digest collaborations and other channels, reveals systematic patterns
in skill degradation. The non-linear acceleration of stroke loss with handicap—each 5-point handicap increase produces disproportionately larger SG deficits—reflects compounding effects rather than
isolated weaknesses. A 20-handicap player is not merely “a 10-handicap who putts worse”; they typically demonstrate degraded performance across all components, with approach play showing the steepest
deterioration due to the technical complexity of consistent ball-striking.
