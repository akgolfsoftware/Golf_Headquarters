**2.1.1 Systematic Failures of Traditional Statistics** Mark Broadie’s development of Strokes Gained emerged from profound dissatisfaction with the analytical tools available to golfers and coaches in the early 2000s. As a professor at Columbia Business School with expertise in quantitative methods, Broadie recognized that the sport’s dominant performance metrics—Fairways **Hit, Greens in Regulation** **(GIR), and Putts Per Round—suffered from structural deficiencies that rendered them not merely** suboptimal but actively misleading for performance improvement. **Fairways Hit exemplifies these failures most starkly.** This binary metric treats all fairway finds as equivalent, ignoring that a fairway bunker or extreme lateral position may be substantially worse than a first-cut rough lie with superior angle and distance. A player hitting 10 fairways at 220 yards appears statistically identical to one hitting 10 fairways at 290 yards, despite the latter’s massive positional advantage in subsequent approach shots. Conversely, a drive one yard into the first cut of rough counts identically to one 30 yards into deep rough or out of bounds—an equivalence that destroys the granular positional information actually determining scoring outcomes.

## Greens in Regulation compounds this error by penalizing close misses while rewarding distant successes.

A ball finishing one yard off the green counts as a missed GIR, while a ball 40 feet from the hole on the putting surface counts as success. The 40-foot putt that follows receives no contextual weighting in traditional putting statistics, despite representing a substantially more difficult scoring proposition than the chip-and-putt from just off the green. GIR also penalizes players who choose aggressive lines to tight pins, rewarding conservative target selection that may actually increase scoring.

## Putts Per Round suffers from the most severe confounding problem. A player with excellent approach

play who consistently leaves 30+ foot putts will record more putts than a player who misses every green and chips close, regardless of actual putting skill. This statistic penalizes good approach play and rewards poor approach play that creates short-game opportunities, making it essentially useless for putting skill assessment without controlling for first-putt distance. Broadie’s research demonstrated that Putts Per Round correlates more strongly with GIR percentage than with actual putting ability—a finding that catalyzed the development of distance-adjusted putting metrics and ultimately the full SG framework.

**2.1.2 Development of SG Methodology** Broadie’s breakthrough leveraged the **PGA Tour** **ShotLink system, operational since 2003, which captures precise ball positions and outcomes for every** shot in every tournament using laser measurement technology. This dataset, comprising over 8 million **shots in the original research and growing continuously, enabled non-parametric regression estimation of** the benchmark function—the expected strokes to hole out from any position defined by distance-to-hole and lie condition. The statistical methodology employed mixed-effects models with two key parameters: µi representing player i’s skill level, and ￿j representing course-round j’s difficulty. This simultaneous estimation was crucial because it prevented confounding: without course difficulty adjustment, players competing at easier courses would appear artificially skilled, while those at difficult venues would seem deficient. The model’s mathematical structure ensures that SG comparisons across tournaments, courses, and seasons remain valid—a property that the AK Platform must preserve in its Norwegian implementation where course variation is extreme.
